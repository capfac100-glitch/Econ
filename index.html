ONE-PAGE NOTE 1: Poverty Fundamentals & Measurement Frameworks

1. Multi-Dimensional Definition of Poverty

  • Overarching Concept: Poverty is defined as the deprivation of multiple dimensions of well-being, including health, education, income, clean environment, social relations, security, and political voice[cite: 1, 3].
  • World Bank Definition: Pronounced deprivation in well-being, comprising:
    1. Inability to acquire basic goods and services necessary for survival with dignity[cite: 1, 3].
    2. Low levels of health and education[cite: 1, 3].
    3. Poor access to clean water and sanitation[cite: 1, 3].
    4. Inadequate physical security[cite: 1, 3].
    5. Lack of voice[cite: 1, 3].
    6. Insufficient capacity and opportunity to improve one's life[cite: 1, 3].

2. Selection Criteria for Measurement Standards

Dimension Choice (Subjective) ──> Criteria Standard (Subjective) ──> Quantifiable Application (Objective)[cite: 1, 3]
  • Evaluating Criteria Options:
    • Wealth: Highly volatile and prone to incorrect reporting[cite: 1, 3].
    • Income: Suffers from incorrect reporting and volatility[cite: 1, 3].
    • Consumption Expenditure (Chosen Metric): Stable over time and yields significantly better reporting accuracy[cite: 1, 3].

3. Poverty Line Basket (PLB) & Measures of Poverty

  • Poverty Line Basket (PLB): Set on the basis of expert opinion to define essential goods and services (food, housing, clothing, transport, education, internet) needed for basic living[cite: 1, 3]. Households that can afford this basket are classified as Non-Poor[cite: 1, 3].
  • Headcount Ratio (HCR): Counts how many are poor (number or percentage of households below the Poverty Line)[cite: 1, 3].
  • Depth of Poverty (Poverty Gap Index): Measures how poor the poor are by calculating the average shortfall distance of poor households from the Poverty Line[cite: 1, 3].
  • Squared Poverty Gap Index (Severity of Poverty): Squares individual poverty gaps: $$\text{Squared Poverty Gap} = \frac{(GAP_1)^2 + (GAP_2)^2 + \dots + (GAP_n)^2}{N}$$ This method is distributionally sensitive and measures the severity of poverty by denoting the extra effort required by a person at the absolute bottom to escape poverty[cite: 1, 3].
Topper's Note: The Headcount Ratio measures absolute incidence, but the Squared Poverty Gap Index is crucial for policy as it measures inequality among the poor[cite: 1, 3].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 2: Poverty Estimation Committees in India

1. Dual Purpose Framework of Measurement

  • National Progress Tracking (Macro): Uses sampling and strict criteria to estimate overall poverty levels (being classified BPL/APL here does not directly impact individuals)[cite: 1, 3].
  • Beneficiary Identification (Micro): Uses targeted scheme-specific criteria (e.g., SECC) to eliminate inclusion and exclusion errors in welfare delivery[cite: 1, 3].

2. Pre-Independence Estimations

  • Dadabhai Naoroji: Formulated the earliest poverty line based on the concept of Jail Cost of Living[cite: 1, 3].
  • National Planning Committee (1938) & Bombay Plan (1944): Initiated structural planning for minimum living standards[cite: 1, 3].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 5: Human Development Framework & Indian Dynamics

1. Human Development Index (HDI) Paradigm

  • 4 Pillars: Equity, Sustainability, Productivity, Empowerment[cite: 1, 3].
  • Approaches: Income, Welfare, Basic Needs (ILO: Health, Education, Food, Water supply, Sanitation, Housing), Capability Approach (Amartya Sen)[cite: 1, 3].
  • HDI Calculation (UNDP HDR): Geometric mean of 3 dimension indices: $$\text{HDI} = \sqrt[3]{H \times E \times S}$$ Health (Life Expectancy at Birth), Education (Mean & Expected Years of Schooling), Standard of Living (GNI per capita PPP)[cite: 1, 3].
  • India HDR 2022 Statistics:
    • HDI Value: 0.644 (Rank 134/193 - Medium HDI)[cite: 1, 3].
    • IHDI (Inequality-adjusted HDI): 0.444 (31.1% loss due to inequality)[cite: 1, 3].
    • GDI (Gender Development Index): \(\frac{\text{Female HDI}}{\text{Male HDI}} = \frac{0.582}{0.684} = 0.862\) (Group 5)[cite: 1, 3].
    • GII (Gender Inequality Index): 0.437 (Rank 108/166)[cite: 1, 3]. Dimensions: Health (MMR, Adolescent birth rate), Empowerment (Parliamentary seats, Secondary education), Labour market participation[cite: 1, 3].

2. India's Poverty Reduction Dynamics

  • Tendulkar Poverty Line Trajectory:

    1950 (56%) → 1993–94 (45%) → 2004–05 (37.2%) → 2009–10 (29.8%) → 2011–12 (21.9%) → 2022–23: 0.9%[cite: 1, 3].

  • World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief (India Updates):
    • $2.15 Line (Extreme Poverty): 16.22% (2011–12) → 2.35% (~33.6M people in 2022–23)[cite: 1, 3].
    • $3.00 Line (2021 PPP): 27.12% (2011–12) → 5.25% (~75.2M people in 2022–23)[cite: 1, 3].
    • Rural Extreme Poverty ($2.15): 18.4% (FY12) → 2.8% (FY21) → 1.1% (FY23)[cite: 1, 3].
    • Urban Extreme Poverty ($2.15): 10.7% (FY12) → 1.1% (FY23)[cite: 1, 3].
    • LMIC Line ($3.65 Line): Declined from 53% to 28.1% in 2022–23 (Rural: 61.8% → 28.1%; Urban: 43.5% → 17.2%)[cite: 1, 3].
    • Key Drivers: 5 States (UP, MP, Maharashtra, WB, Bihar) hosted 65% of extreme poor in 2011–12 and contributed \(\frac{2}{3}\text{rd}\) of overall poverty reduction by 2022–23[cite: 1, 3].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 6: Inequality Concepts, Theories & Metrics

1. Causes & Theories of Inequality

  • Manifestations: Economic, Social, Political[cite: 1, 3]. Driven by natural factors, political access, and the Resource Curse (resource-rich areas failing to develop due to misgovernance and fractured society)[cite: 1, 3].
  • Marxist Theory (Karl Marx - Das Kapital): Society splits into Bourgeoisie (capital/land owners) and Proletariat (laborers)[cite: 1, 3]. Rate of return on capital exceeds return on labor (\(r > g\)), causing profits to grow exponentially while wages remain stagnant[cite: 1, 3].
  • Modern Economic Theory: Contradicts Marx over the long run; wages and profits grow at comparable rates[cite: 1, 3]. Short-term inequality stems from failure of firm survival, entry barriers, unfair practices, and lack of Rule of Law[cite: 1, 3].

2. Quantitative Measurement of Inequality

  • Lorenz Curve: Graphs cumulative percentage of total national income against cumulative percentage of population[cite: 1, 3].
  • Gini Coefficient: Derived from the Lorenz Curve: $$\text{Gini Coefficient} = \frac{a}{a + b}$$ Where \(a\) is the area between Line of Absolute Equality and Lorenz Curve, and \(a+b\) is the total area under the Line of Equality[cite: 1, 3].
    • \(\text{Gini} = 0\): Perfect Equality[cite: 1, 3].
    • \(\text{Gini} = 1\): Perfect Inequality[cite: 1, 3].
  • India's Consumption Gini Index: Improved from 28.8 (2011–12) to 25.5 (2022–23) based on HCES data, placing India among the world's most equal societies in consumption[cite: 1, 3].

3. Growth Models Comparison

Trickle-Down Model: Focus High Growth ──> Collect Tax Revenue ──> Redistribution to Passive Recipients[cite: 1, 3]
Inclusive Growth Model: Skill & Capacity Building ──> Jobs & Opportunities ──> Active Economic Contributors[cite: 1, 3]

ONE-PAGE NOTE 7: Unemployment Fundamentals & Measurement Metrics

1. Core Concepts & Demographic Framework

  • Unemployment Definition: Refers to people who are capable of working and actively seeking work, but are unable to find work[cite: 2].
  • Working Age Population: Defined in terms of age only[cite: 2]. Varies across texts; ILO benchmark is 15–59 years (or 18–59 years)[cite: 2]. $$\text{Dependents} = \text{Population below 15 years} + \text{Population above 60 years} \text{[cite: 2]}$$ $$\text{Dependency Ratio} = \frac{\text{Non-Working Age Population (Dependents)}}{\text{Working Age Population}} \text{[cite: 2]}$$
  • Labour Force & Work Force Metrics:
    • Labour Force (LF): Includes working-age population capable of working and actively seeking work (Employed + Unemployed)[cite: 2].
    • Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR): $$\text{LFPR} = \frac{\text{Labour Force}}{\text{Total Population}} \text{[cite: 2]}$$
    • Worker Population Ratio (WPR): $$\text{WPR} = \frac{\text{Total Workers}}{\text{Total Population}} \times 1000 \text{[cite: 2]}$$
    • Unemployment Rate (UR): $$\text{Unemployment Rate} = \frac{\text{No. of Unemployed Persons}}{\text{Labour Force}} \text{[cite: 2]}$$

2. Demographic Dividend & Policy Dilemma

  • Demographic Dividend: Expected economic growth derived from a favorable population structure (young & healthy working-age population)[cite: 2]. India's median age is 29 years, offering a window of opportunity up to 2055–2060[cite: 2].
  • Demographic Dividend vs. Disaster: High working-age population + Skilling + Employment = Demographic Dividend[cite: 2]. No skilling + No job opportunities = Demographic Disaster[cite: 2].

3. Unemployment Measurement Methodologies in India

  • Measuring Agencies: MoSPI (NSSO / CSO / NSO / NSC) conducts sample surveys using representative samples across regions, gender, and caste[cite: 2]. Randomised Control Trials (RCTs) are used in sampling to minimize bias[cite: 2].
  • Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Approaches:
    • Usual Status (PS + SS): Combines Principal Status (in LF > 182 days) and Subsidiary Status (worked > 30 days)[cite: 2]. Represents the headline unemployment measure[cite: 2].
    • Current Weekly Status (CWS): Person is employed if engaged in work for at least 1 hour on any single day in the preceding 7 days[cite: 2]. Captures seasonal fluctuations[cite: 2].
    • Current Daily Status (CDS): Measures daily intensity (4+ hours = full day; 1–4 hours = half day; < 1 hour = unemployed)[cite: 2]. Used by CMIE[cite: 2].
Measurement Rigor (Intensity): Unemployment Rate (CDS) > UR (CWS) > UR (Usual Status)[cite: 2]

ONE-PAGE NOTE 8: Types of Unemployment & Sectoral Growth Dynamics

1. Types & Taxonomy of Unemployment

Type Defining Characteristics Interventionist / Market Solutions
Structural UE Mismatch between skills demanded and possessed due to structural shifts in the economy (e.g., Agri to Services, Petrol to EVs, AI adoption)[cite: 2]. Caused by occupational, industrial, or geographical immobility[cite: 2]. Interventionist: Education reform, skilling/retraining subsidies, relocation incentives[cite: 2].
Free Market: Reducing strict wage rigidities[cite: 2].
Seasonal UE Unemployment due to seasonal patterns in industries like agriculture, tourism, and construction[cite: 2]. Encouraging off-season employment, multi-cropping, adapting unemployment insurance[cite: 2].
Frictional UE Temporary unemployment while transitioning between jobs or entering the job market[cite: 2]. Caused by information asymmetry[cite: 2]. Improving job portals, public employment exchanges, transparent information dissemination[cite: 2].
Disguised UE Surplus labor where the marginal productivity of additional workers is zero ($MP_L = 0$)[cite: 2]. Commonly seen in agriculture[cite: 2]. Shifting excess labor from agriculture to manufacturing and labor-intensive services[cite: 2].
Voluntary UE Individuals choosing to stay unemployed despite job offers due to low dignity of labor, waiting for better offers, or lucrative benefits[cite: 2]. Rationalizing reservation wages, improving job matching quality[cite: 2].

2. Growth-Employment Linkages & The "Jobless Growth" Debate

  • Employment Elasticity: Responsiveness of employment growth relative to GDP growth: $$\text{Employment Elasticity} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Employment}}{\% \text{ Change in GDP Growth}} \text{[cite: 2]}$$ High elasticity denotes labor-intensive growth; low elasticity denotes productivity/capital-intensive growth[cite: 2]. India's overall employment elasticity dropped from 0.57 (1970s) to ~0.18–0.20 (2000s)[cite: 2].
  • Critical Assessment of Jobless Growth:
    • If employment growth rate = population growth rate, Unemployment Rate remains constant[cite: 2].
    • Graduate employability gap: Economic Survey 2023-24 highlights that only 51% of graduates are employable[cite: 2]. High youth unemployment often reflects skill mismatch and voluntary waiting for high-aspiration jobs[cite: 2].

3. Structural Transformation Trajectory in India

  • GDP Share vs Employment Share:
    • Agriculture: GDP share declined from 59% (1950) to ~15%[cite: 2]; Employment share remains disproportionately high at ~43–45%[cite: 2]. Low employment elasticity[cite: 2].
    • Industry/Manufacturing: GDP share ~27–30%[cite: 2]; Employment share ~23–25%[cite: 2]. High employment elasticity (engine of labor absorption)[cite: 2].
    • Services: GDP share ~55–60%[cite: 2]; Employment share ~30%[cite: 2]. Low employment elasticity (productivity-driven growth)[cite: 2].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 10: Formalisation, Dwarfism & Labour Law Deregulation

1. Dualism in the Indian Labour Market

  • Organised vs. Unorganised Sectors: Organised firms are registered enterprises governed by formal statutory provisions[cite: 2]. Unorganised firms operate with low regulatory visibility[cite: 2].
  • Formal vs. Informal Employment:
    • Formal Employment: Employment with well-defined contracts, employment rights, social security (EPF, ESI), and statutory protection[cite: 2].
    • Informal Employment: Work without formal contracts, guaranteed benefits, or social security, existing across both unorganised and organised firms[cite: 2].
  • NCEUS Criteria for Informality: Firms employing < 10 workers (without electricity) or < 20 workers (with electricity) are categorized as informal[cite: 2].

2. Perverse Incentives & "Voluntary Dwarfism"

  • The Dwarfism Phenomenon: Stringent compliance requirements (labour regulations, environmental norms, tax audits) create heavy compliance costs as firms grow larger[cite: 2].
  • Consequence: Small firms choose to "stay small" (voluntary dwarfism) or rely heavily on contract workers rather than hiring regular formal workers[cite: 2].
    Key Statistic (ASI FY23): 41% of workers in the formal manufacturing sector are employed on a contract basis[cite: 2]. Small firms (< 100 workers) constitute 80% of formal manufacturing firms but contribute only 14% to employment[cite: 2].
  • Policy Shift: Economic Survey emphasizes the need to "nourish dwarfs to become giants" by replacing size-based incentives with time-bound (sunset clause) incentives[cite: 2].

3. Rationalisation of Wages & Wage Types

  • Minimum Wage: Legally mandated wage level set by authorities, varying by region, skill level, and scheduled employments[cite: 2]. (India has historically had 1,915 scheduled minimum wage categories)[cite: 2].
  • Floor Wage: Statutory minimum threshold below which no state government can set its minimum wage[cite: 2].
  • Living Wage: Subjective benchmark required for a worker to maintain a dignified standard of living (housing, health, education)[cite: 2].
  • Fair Wage: Wage positioned between minimum wage and living wage, balancing employer capacity with worker welfare[cite: 2].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 11: Workforce Classifications, Gig Economy & Minimum Wages

1. Official Workforce Classifications (Census & NSO)

  • Census Categories (Occupational):
    • Cultivators: Cultivate self-owned or leased land[cite: 2].
    • Agricultural Labourers: Work on another person's land for cash/kind wages without lease/risk[cite: 2].
    • Household Industry Workers: Engage in home-based processing/production[cite: 2].
    • Other Workers: All remaining economic workers (govt servants, factory workers, teachers, trade/services)[cite: 2].
  • Census Work Intensity: Main Worker (works $\ge 183$ days/year) vs. Marginal Worker (works < 183 days/year)[cite: 2].
  • NSO Employment Status Categories (PLFS 2023-24):
    • Self-Employed: Own-account workers, employers, and unpaid helpers in household enterprises[cite: 2].
      • Rural: Male 59.4%, Female 73.5%[cite: 2] | Urban: Male 39.8%, Female 42.3%[cite: 2].
    • Regular Wage/Salaried: Recurrent wages/salaries[cite: 2].
      • Rural: Male 15.8%, Female 7.8%[cite: 2] | Urban: Male 46.8%, Female 49.4%[cite: 2].
    • Casual Labour: Daily or periodic contract work[cite: 2].
      • Rural: Male 24.9%, Female 18.7%[cite: 2] | Urban: Male 13.4%, Female 8.3%[cite: 2].

2. Gig & Platform Economy

  • Gig Worker: A person who performs work or participates in a work arrangement outside the traditional employer-employee relationship (e.g., freelancers, part-time professors)[cite: 2].
  • Platform Worker: A worker whose work arrangement is accessed through an online platform/aggregator (e.g., delivery partners, ride-sharing drivers)[cite: 2]. All platform workers are gig workers, but not all gig workers are platform workers[cite: 2].

3. Card & Krueger Natural Experiment (Nobel Prize 2021)

  • Core Finding: Classical economic theory suggested that raising minimum wages reduces employment[cite: 2]. Card & Krueger examined New Jersey's minimum wage hike (from $4.25 to $5.05) vs. Pennsylvania[cite: 2].
  • Empirical Result: Raising the minimum wage did not lead to job losses[cite: 2]. It created a "lighthouse effect," boosting low-end consumption without harming employment elasticity[cite: 2].

ONE-PAGE NOTE 12: Consolidation into the 4 Labour Codes

Overview: Amalgamation of 29 Central Labour Laws into 4 Codes

1. Code on Wages, 2019

  • Consolidates: 4 laws (Equal Remuneration Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Wages Act, Payment of Bonus Act)[cite: 2].
  • Key Provision: Introduces a statutory National Floor Level Minimum Wage set by the Central Government[cite: 2]. States cannot set minimum wages below this floor wage[cite: 2]. Universalizes minimum wage protection to all workers across formal and informal sectors[cite: 2].

2. Code on Social Security, 2020

  • Consolidates: 9 laws (EPF Act, ESI Act, Maternity Benefit Act, Gratuity Act, etc.)[cite: 2].
  • Social Security Provisions: Empowers Central/State governments to notify welfare funds for gig, platform, and unorganised workers[cite: 2]. Aggregators must contribute 1–2% of their annual turnover toward social security schemes[cite: 2].
  • Institutional Framework: Central Board of Trustees (EPF), ESIC, and National/State Social Security Boards[cite: 2].

3. Industrial Relations Code, 2020

  • Consolidates: 3 laws (Industrial Disputes Act, Trade Unions Act, Industrial Employment Standing Orders Act)[cite: 2].
  • Negotiating Union: Requires a single trade union with $\ge 51\%$ worker support to act as the sole negotiating union[cite: 2]. If multiple unions exist, a negotiating council is formed with unions having $\ge 20\%$ membership[cite: 2].
  • Standing Orders Threshold: Raised from 100 workers to 300 workers[cite: 2]. Establishments with up to 300 workers can lay off, retrench, or close without prior government approval[cite: 2].

4. Code on Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSHWC), 2020

  • Consolidates: 13 laws governing workplace safety and health[cite: 2].
  • Coverage: Applies to factories with at least 20 workers (with power) or 40 workers (without power)[cite: 2].
  • Inter-State Migrant Workers: Expands definition to include workers who move independently across states for employment earning up to ₹18,000/month[cite: 2]. Ensures portability of benefits (PDS ration, building construction welfare fund) and journey allowance[cite: 2].
  • Working Hours & Gender Rights: Standardizes daily work hours to a maximum of 8 hours/day[cite: 2]. Grants legal right to women to work in all establishments at night with their explicit consent and safety provisions[cite: 2].
